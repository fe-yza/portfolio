Overview

Banking apps aren't built for a student's money

Most banking dashboards are built for a general adult user — a mortgage, a car payment, a retirement fund. A student's financial life looks different: irregular income, small recurring costs, and savings goals sized in the hundreds, not the thousands — a trip, a gadget, a gift. Squeezed into a generic dashboard, those goals get buried under features that don't apply yet.

This concept keeps the trusted visual language of a bank's app — the red, the density, the credibility — but restructures the information around what a student checks daily: how much is left, and how close am I to what I'm saving for.

How might wehelp students see their spending clearly and stay motivated toward small, specific savings goals?

Key screens

Where the redesign does the most work

01 · Home dashboard

Every number framed as a decision, not just data

The home screen leads with a personal greeting and a single spending figure — but never in isolation. Every number is framed against something: typical spending, a budget ceiling, a goal's finish line.

  • $437 vs. "typical spending" — turns a raw total into a comparison, so a student can tell if this month is normal without doing the math.
  • Budget bar at 79% reads as color and length before it reads as a number, so status is legible at a glance.
  • Savings goals preview surfaces three goals with progress bars right on the dashboard — no separate screen needed just to feel the momentum.
02 · Savings goals overview

Every goal is its own small win in progress

Zooming into savings turns three progress bars into full cards — each with a name, a dollar milestone, and a status written in plain language rather than a raw percentage alone.

  • "Add a new goal" sits first on the page, always the easiest next action rather than buried in a menu.
  • Status lines like "You're ahead by 3 months" translate a progress bar into something worth feeling good about.
  • Overflow menu (•••) keeps edit and pause actions available without cluttering the primary view.
03 · Add a new goal

Three decisions, not a spreadsheet

Setting up a goal asks for a name and a target, grounds it in a real account, then asks the one question that determines whether the habit sticks: how does the money actually get there?

  • Placeholder examples ("Emergency Fund") lower the blank-page problem of naming a goal from scratch.
  • Automatic weekly transfer is the default, expanded and pre-filled — manual saving is available, but never the assumed choice.
  • "Move leftover budget at month-end" turns unused spending money into savings without asking the student to remember to do it.

Visual system

A white canvas, one red signal

The system stays deliberately restrained — white surfaces, near-black text, and a single confident red reserved for headers and primary actions. No gradients, no secondary accent competing for attention: red always means "this is the important thing on the screen."

Reflection

What I learned, and where it goes next

Takeaways

  • Comparison beats raw numbers. "$57 above typical spending" did more work than the $437 total ever could on its own.
  • Small goals need their own language. A $150 gift and a $900 trip both deserve a status line, not just a percentage.
  • Defaults are a design decision. Making automatic transfer the pre-filled option nudges behavior more than any copy could.

Next steps

  • Usability testing with current students to validate goal sizes and whether the automatic-transfer default feels safe.
  • Accessibility pass on red/white contrast ratios across every screen, especially small status text.
  • Shared goals — letting a group split a savings goal, like a trip planned with friends.
